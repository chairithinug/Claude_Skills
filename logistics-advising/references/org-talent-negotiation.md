# Organization, Talent, Incentives, and Negotiation

This is the soft layer that determines whether the framework recommendations actually get executed. Senior practitioners know that org design and incentives kill or save more transformations than process design. Negotiation is the daily skill the skill's main body neglects.

## Why this layer matters

A common pattern: an organization invests in S&OP, hires a digital transformation lead, buys a TMS, runs an AI pilot — and the metrics don't move. Diagnosis is almost never that the framework was wrong. Diagnosis is that:
- The supply chain function reports to the wrong executive
- Sales incentives still reward order-taking volume regardless of fulfillment cost
- Procurement is measured on PPV (purchase price variance), not total landed cost
- The S&OP meeting is attended by junior planners, not the people who can actually trade off
- The "control tower" was built without authority over the people it was meant to coordinate

These are org problems wearing process clothing.

## Where supply chain reports — and why it matters

### Common reporting structures
- **Under COO** — operational integration; common in manufacturing-heavy organizations
- **Under CFO** — working-capital and cost focus; common in distribution-heavy and CPG
- **Under CEO** (CSCO as direct report) — strategic centrality; common in mature, network-design-focused organizations (Apple, Walmart, Amazon)
- **Under Sales/Commercial** — service-level focus; common in B2B distribution; usually under-invests in cost
- **Split** — procurement under CFO, planning under COO, distribution under sales — common but creates the silos that S&OP is supposed to bridge

### What changes by structure
- Reporting under CFO tends to over-prioritize working capital reduction; service-level erosion follows
- Reporting under sales tends to over-prioritize service; inventory and freight cost balloon
- Reporting under COO is balanced but can lose strategic visibility
- Reporting to CEO (CSCO direct) signals priority and unlocks cross-functional authority — but isn't always a fit

### Implication for advisory
When a recommendation requires cross-functional trade-offs and the org doesn't have an executive who can make them, the recommendation will fail regardless of analytical quality. Naming this in the diagnosis is sometimes the most useful thing a logistics advisor can do.

## Operating models and centers of excellence

### The CoE pattern
A Supply Chain Center of Excellence consolidates expertise (planning, network design, analytics, vendor management) and serves business units as an internal consultancy. Common in firms with multiple BUs or geographies.

- **Pro**: scarce expertise leveraged broadly; consistent methods; talent magnet
- **Con**: BU teams resist; CoE recommendations get watered down at execution; CoE talent disconnected from execution reality

### The Control Tower pattern
A function that monitors end-to-end execution in real time, intervenes on exceptions, coordinates across regions or BUs.

- **Pro**: closes the visibility-to-action gap that visibility platforms alone don't
- **Con**: works only with delegated authority; without it, becomes a reporting desk
- **Often confused with**: a real-time visibility dashboard. They're different — a tower has people who *act*, a dashboard does not.

### The Federated / Hybrid pattern
Strategic capability centralized; execution decentralized. Common in mature global organizations.

- Network design, S&OP architecture, vendor master agreements: centralized
- Daily execution, local carrier relationships, customer service: decentralized
- Requires clear interfaces and decision rights — the failure mode is muddy boundaries

### The Lean / Embedded pattern
No corporate function; supply chain expertise embedded in BUs. Common in holding-company structures and very large firms with autonomous BUs.

- Loses scale economies and consistency
- Speed and BU ownership advantages
- Requires shared data and KPI standards or comparison becomes impossible

## Roles and titles in modern supply chain organizations

The senior bench has evolved. A 2026 organization typically has:

- **Chief Supply Chain Officer (CSCO)** or **EVP Supply Chain** — strategic owner
- **VP / Senior Director Planning** — S&OP / IBP, demand and supply planning
- **VP / Senior Director Procurement** or **CPO** — supplier-facing; sometimes split commodity vs indirect
- **VP / Senior Director Logistics / Fulfillment** — transportation, warehousing, customer service
- **Senior Director Network Design / Supply Chain Strategy** — modeling, capacity planning
- **Senior Director Trade Compliance / Customs** — regulatory; often under General Counsel rather than supply chain in regulated industries
- **Senior Director Supply Chain Risk / Resilience** — newer; rising importance post-COVID
- **Director Sustainability / Decarbonization in Supply Chain** — Scope 3 reporting, vendor engagement, carbon program
- **Director Supply Chain Analytics / Data Science** — increasingly central; bridges to enterprise data and AI teams
- **Director / Manager Customer Logistics or Customer Supply Chain** — for B2B operations with major retail or industrial customers (CPG selling to Walmart has dedicated Walmart-customer logistics teams)

## Incentive design — where strategies live or die

### Common incentive misalignments
- **Sales** measured on revenue / order volume; takes any order regardless of fulfillment cost
- **Procurement** measured on PPV (purchase price variance) against budget; ignores quality, lead time, freight
- **Planning** measured on forecast accuracy in isolation; ignores planning-induced bullwhip
- **Logistics** measured on cost per unit; ignores service level
- **Manufacturing** measured on plant utilization; ignores changeovers and inventory

When these are siloed, S&OP fails because each function defends its local optimum.

### Better incentive patterns
- **Shared metrics across functions** — perfect order rate or OTIF as a shared bonus driver across sales, planning, logistics
- **Total cost ownership** — procurement measured on total landed cost, not unit price
- **Working-capital incentives** — DSO/DIO targets cascading from finance to commercial and supply chain
- **Service-level shared accountability** — fill rate or PoR shared between commercial and supply chain
- **Long-term capability metrics** — talent retention, capability development, vendor health

These don't write themselves. The senior question is not "what should the metric be" but "who is willing to be measured on it" — and that's a political question.

## Talent — the supply chain workforce

### Skills currently scarce (high market value)
- Network design and supply chain modeling (Anylogic, Llamasoft / Coupa, Optilogic)
- Advanced demand planning with ML (not just forecasting buttons in SAP/o9)
- Trade compliance with technical depth (HS, RoO, sanctions, export control)
- Supply chain finance and trade finance knowledge in operations roles
- Sustainability and Scope 3 emissions accounting
- Data engineering and analytics within supply chain (separate from corporate IT)
- Solid execution leadership in cross-border and customs (under-supplied in many growth markets)

### Skills commoditizing
- Basic forecasting (tooling is doing more of this; planner role evolving toward exception handling and judgment)
- Pure freight procurement (digital benchmarking has flattened this)
- Manual order management (workflow automation is eating it)

### Certifications worth pursuing
- **APICS / ASCM** — CPIM (production and inventory management), CSCP (supply chain professional), CLTD (logistics, transportation, distribution)
- **CSCMP** — SCPro (Supply Chain Professional certification)
- **CILT** — Chartered Institute of Logistics and Transport — UK and Commonwealth recognition
- **CIPS** — Chartered Institute of Procurement and Supply
- **Customs broker license** — country-specific (US Customs Broker, Thai customs broker, etc.) — gateway to compliance careers
- **AEO consultant** accreditations — country-specific
- **Six Sigma / Lean** — broadly recognized but not supply-chain specific
- **GLEC** trained practitioner for emissions accounting

### Talent pipeline reality in Thailand and ASEAN
- Strong undergraduate logistics programs at Thai universities (Chulalongkorn, Mahidol, KMUTT, Burapha) and across ASEAN
- Mid-career experienced practitioners are scarce — many move to MNCs and stay
- Customs and trade compliance senior bench is particularly thin
- Cross-border bilingual practitioners (Thai-Burmese, Thai-Khmer) are valuable for GMS corridor work
- Digital and AI skills bridging supply chain and data science still rare — opportunity for someone like Mew

## Negotiation — the daily skill the framework misses

A logistics professional's calendar is full of negotiations: with carriers, 3PLs, customs brokers, suppliers, internal finance. Best practices here are not generic.

### Carrier RFP cycle
Annual or biennial in most large shippers. Typical sequence:
1. **Bid preparation** — lane data cleansing (most shippers' lane data is not clean enough to bid accurately), volume forecasting, current-state baselining
2. **Carrier qualification** — financial health, equipment availability, technology compatibility, AEO/safety record
3. **RFI** — non-binding capability solicitation
4. **RFP** — detailed lane-by-lane bid; pricing, capacity, service commitments
5. **Optimization** — running multiple award scenarios (lowest cost, balanced, single-source vs split)
6. **Award and contracting** — terms negotiation, MQC commitments, termination provisions
7. **Implementation and stewardship** — onboarding, integration, performance monitoring

Common failure: bidding on dirty lane data. The carrier prices to expected reality, not the data submitted; the shipper signs a contract that doesn't match operations; surcharges accumulate.

### Contract clauses that matter
Beyond rate sheets:
- **Bunker / fuel adjustment formula** — define index and frequency precisely
- **Currency adjustment** — for cross-border and international lanes
- **Peak season surcharge mechanics** — notification period, magnitude caps
- **Minimum quantity commitment (MQC)** — failure consequences, force majeure carve-outs
- **Equipment availability commitments** — recourse for non-availability
- **Detention and demurrage free time and rates** — non-trivial annual exposure
- **Accessorial caps** — many accessorial charges (reweigh, reconsignment, layover) are disputed; cap them
- **Audit rights** — explicit right to audit billing for 12+ months retroactive
- **Termination for cause / convenience** — escape provisions
- **Performance metrics and remedies** — credits or termination if OTD or claims thresholds breached
- **Insurance and liability limits** — Carmack Amendment in US; international convention regimes (Hague-Visby, Hamburg, Rotterdam, CMR for road, Montreal for air); shipper-vs-carrier liability allocation
- **Data ownership and confidentiality** — increasingly material as carriers commercialize shipment data

### 3PL contract specifics
3PLs add operational scope beyond freight:
- **Cost-plus vs fixed-price vs gain-share** — each has incentive implications
- **Open-book / closed-book** — visibility into 3PL margins; cost-plus is open-book by definition
- **Performance bonds** — for high-value or high-volume relationships
- **Service-level agreements** with credits
- **Right to insourcing** — termination provisions if you decide to bring back in-house
- **IT and system access** — your data, their systems; ownership and exit provisions
- **People retention** — key personnel clauses; non-poach provisions

### Carrier-relationship management
Best practice across the industry:
- **Quarterly business reviews** with major carriers — performance, market context, mutual roadmap
- **Annual strategic reviews** with executives — relationship-level, not transactional
- **Joint scorecards** — agreed metrics, jointly reviewed
- **Capacity commitments in capacity-tight markets** — relationship deposits that pay back when the market turns
- **Carrier-side perspective awareness** — what's hard for them; what makes you a preferred shipper

### Internal negotiation
Often the harder negotiation. Examples:
- **With finance** — defending working-capital choices that look expensive on inventory but save freight
- **With sales** — pushing back on commitments to customers that fulfillment can't sustainably deliver
- **With manufacturing** — coordinating production sequence with shipping windows
- **With IT** — getting development resources for supply chain priorities

The pattern that works: speak in the other function's language and metrics. Finance responds to working capital and ROIC; sales responds to revenue and customer retention; manufacturing responds to plant utilization and changeover cost. Frame the supply chain ask in those terms.

## Practitioner truth

Most logistics organizations under-invest in this layer because it doesn't have software vendors selling it. There's no SaaS for "negotiate a better contract" or "redesign your incentive structure." But the ROI on cleaning up incentive misalignment, getting reporting structure right, and improving negotiation discipline often dwarfs the ROI on the latest tooling investment. A skill that ignores this layer is technically competent but practically incomplete.
